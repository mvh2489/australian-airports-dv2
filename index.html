01 / THE AIRPORT NETWORK

A few gateways carry a lot of the traffic.

Australia's population centres are widely separated. Its largest airports connect those cities, while smaller airports extend the network along the coast and into the interior.

Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane account for more than half of national domestic airport movements. Concentration is the first feature of this network: a few gateways handle a large share of activity.

02 / DISRUPTION & RECOVERY

The shock was shared. The recovery was not.

COVID-19 interrupted years of growth. International passenger trips fell especially sharply. By 2024–25, national traffic had recovered much of the lost ground, but individual airports followed different paths.

The shock was much deeper internationally.

Between 2018–19 and 2020–21, international passenger trips fell about 97%, compared with 65% domestically. These are national totals: the airport heatmap below reveals how the disruption was distributed, using each airport's own pre-pandemic baseline.

Recovery depends on where you look.

Perth's combined traffic was about 14.5% above its 2018–19 level in 2024–25. Canberra remained about 12.9% below.

The national recovery therefore hides different local outcomes. A smaller airport can regain its earlier volume while a larger one still has more passengers missing in absolute terms. The heatmap compares proportional recovery; this plot shows the size of the gap.

These volumes do not establish the causes of recovery. The Yearbook airport figures are rounded to the nearest thousand movements.

03 / BEYOND THE BIG CITIES

Busy is relative.

A large state population often brings large passenger totals. Dividing activity by population reveals a different geography: less populous states and territories can have high airport activity relative to their resident base.

04 / THE CONNECTIONS

Airports matter because of what they connect.

Passengers travel along routes, not just through dots on a map. The Melbourne–Sydney corridor carries the most domestic passenger trips, but the network also connects regional centres to the capital cities.

Busy does not necessarily mean full.

A route can carry many passengers because it offers many flights, many seats per flight, or high seat utilisation. These are separate dimensions. The scatterplot shows passenger and aircraft activity; the seating chart shows how much capacity was used.

Likewise, the three maps answer different questions: where activity occurs, how it compares with population, and which places are connected. Together they show a concentrated network with very different local scales and recovery paths.

SOURCES & METHODS

A note on the numbers.

Government statistics

  • BITRE Domestic Aviation Activity, 2025–26: Tables 1–5 and 7, published pages 3–11 and 23. Airport and route comparisons use financial years ending June 2025 and June 2026.
  • BITRE Australian Infrastructure and Transport Statistics Yearbook 2025: Tables 6.1, 6.2, 6.3a and 6.3b, published pages 138–141. Historical airport comparisons include domestic and international services.
  • ABS National, State and Territory Population, March 2026 release: Table 4, Persons by state and territory. The December 2025 observations are used to match the midpoint of 2025–26.

Geography and preparation

  • OurAirports supplies airport coordinates and state identifiers. Thursday Island is plotted at its serving Horn Island airport. Burnie is plotted at Wynyard airport.
  • Natural Earth supplies simplified state boundaries. Both geographic datasets are public domain. All three maps use an Australia-centred Albers equal-area projection.
  • Source values are retained; figures in thousands are converted to counts. Derived rates, shares and comparisons are documented in Data notes. No missing values are filled with invented figures.

Coverage: 58 distinct airports, formed by deduplicating BITRE's top-ten and top-fifty-regional tables. They represent 98.1% of national domestic airport passenger movements in 2025–26. “Regional” in the source follows ABS geography; this page does not assume that all state capitals are non-regional. All financial-year labels refer to 1 July–30 June. Historical tables and the latest report can differ because of revisions and rounding.

Design assets: original SVG aircraft, airport and route illustrations created for this project. Nunito and DM Sans are from Google Fonts and bundled under their SIL Open Font Licenses.

Charts were created using Vega and Vega-Lite. Generative AI (ChatGPT) was used to assist with layout planning, gaining stylistic feedback on draft webpages, refinding body text to accompany charts and in creating aviation based illustrations.
